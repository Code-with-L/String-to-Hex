Glossary

44 terms, grouped by area.

Electrical

Voltage
Electrical voltage is the potential difference between two points in an electric field. It is the "pressure" that pushes charge around a circuit.
Electric current
Current is the amount of charge passing a point in a circuit per second. 1 ampere is one coulomb per second.
Electrical resistance
Resistance measures how much a component opposes current. 1 ohm allows 1 ampere to flow when 1 volt is applied across it.
Electric power
Power is the rate of energy transfer. 1 watt is one joule per second.
Electric charge
Charge is a conserved property; electrons carry a negative charge of magnitude 1.602176565×10⁻¹⁹ C.
Capacitance
Capacitance is the ratio of stored charge to the voltage across it. 1 farad stores 1 coulomb at 1 volt.
Inductance
Inductance is the ratio of induced voltage to the rate of change of current through a coil.
Impedance
For AC circuits, impedance generalises resistance: it accounts for reactive components and the phase shift they introduce.
Conductance
Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance. 1 siemens is one reciprocal ohm.
Power factor
Power factor is the ratio of real power (kW) to apparent power (kVA). 1.0 is perfect; below 1 the extra current is wasted as heat.
Apparent power
Apparent power is voltage times current regardless of phase. Utilities bill against real power, but size equipment against apparent power.
Reactance
Capacitive reactance falls with frequency; inductive reactance rises with it.
RMS voltage
For a sine wave the RMS value is the peak divided by √2. This is why a 230 V supply is quoted as 230 V and not 325 V.
Power efficiency
Efficiency is the ratio of useful output power to input power. It can never exceed 100% — the rest becomes heat, sound and motion.

Circuits

Series connection
In a series circuit the same current flows through every component, and the total voltage is the sum of the individual drops.
Parallel connection
In a parallel circuit every branch sees the same voltage, and the branch currents sum to the total.
Voltage divider
With resistors in series, the drop across one resistor is proportional to its share of the total resistance.
Kirchhoff's voltage law
Energy is conserved, so the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero. Sign convention: rises positive, drops negative.
Kirchhoff's current law
No charge accumulates at an ideal node. The algebraic sum of currents at any junction is zero.
Coulomb's law
The electrostatic force between two charges is proportional to their product and falls off with the square of the distance between them.
Superconductivity
Below its critical temperature a superconductor conducts with no electrical resistance and excludes magnetic flux.

Units

Ohm (Ω)
Named after Georg Ohm. 1 Ω is the resistance that allows 1 A to flow across 1 V.
Watt (W)
Named after James Watt. 1 W is one joule per second.
Volt (V)
One volt is the potential difference that moves one coulomb of charge per coulomb of work done.
Ampere (A)
Since the 2019 SI redefinition it is defined by the elementary charge, not by a prototype.
Farad (F)
One farad stores one coulomb at one volt.
Henry (H)
One henry induces one volt across a coil when current through it changes at one ampere per second.
Decibel (dB)
Decibels express ratios, not absolute quantities. Ten decibels is a factor of ten in power; twenty is a factor of a hundred.
dB-milliwatt (dBm)
0 dBm is exactly 1 mW. Because the reference is fixed, dBm is an absolute measure you can compare directly across equipment.
dB-watt (dBW)
0 dBW is 1 W, so dBm = dBW + 30 at all times.
Kilowatt (kW)
Residential and commercial billing is in kilowatt-hours, and equipment is often rated in kilowatts.
Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
One kilowatt-hour is the energy of running a 1 kW load for one hour. It is a unit of energy, and bills are priced per kWh.
Kilovolt-amp (kVA)
Equipment is rated in kVA because transformers and cables are sized by apparent power, not real power.

Mathematics

Logarithm
log_b(x) is the power b must be raised to in order to equal x. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition, which is why it appears wherever things scale exponentially.
Exponential function
In y = aˣ the output is multiplied by a for every unit increase in x. Decay is the same function with a < 1.

Computing

Radix / base
Base 2 uses {0,1}, base 16 uses {0–9,A–F}, base 36 uses {0–9,A–Z}. Positional notation means each digit is multiplied by the base raised to its position.
Bit
A bit is one 0 or one 1. It is a count, not a piece of data on its own.
Byte
One byte has 256 possible values, 0 to 255, which is exactly one hex digit pair. This is why hex is the natural way to read binary.
Hexadecimal
Each hex digit encodes exactly four binary bits, so a byte is always two hex digits. Nothing is lost in either direction.
UTF-8
UTF-8 stores ASCII as one byte and uses two to four bytes for everything else, so plain English costs no more than ASCII.
ASCII
ASCII covers 0–127: control codes, space, and printable English. Anything beyond it needs a wider encoding.
Checksum
A checksum is cheap to compute and compare. A CRC is a checksum with good error detection; it is not a defence against deliberate tampering.
Byte order (endianness)
Big-endian puts the most significant byte first, matching how numbers are written. Little-endian puts it last, which suits little-endian CPUs.
Cyclic redundancy check
A CRC treats a message as a polynomial, divides by a fixed generator polynomial, and sends the remainder. Bursts of errors are detected reliably.
