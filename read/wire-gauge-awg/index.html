Wire Gauge (AWG & SWG)

Why American Wire Gauge numbers get smaller as wires get thicker, and what the numbers actually mean.

AWG is a logarithmic scale: each step in gauge number is a fixed percentage change in diameter, and therefore a fixed ratio change in cross-sectional area and resistance.

d(in) = 0.005 · 92^((36 − n)/39) d in inches, n the AWG number. d(mm) = 0.127 · 92^((36 − n)/39).

The counter-intuitive direction is worth internalising: 14 AWG is thicker and carries more current than 18 AWG, because 18 is the higher number. A wire 40 AWG above a given gauge is roughly half the diameter.

Working out area

A = π/4 · d² Cross-section in in² from inches, or mm² from millimetres.

Working backwards from a diameter

n = 36 − 39 · log₉₂(d / 0.005)

British Standard Wire Gauge

SWG is an older, non-logarithmic series defined by a table of diameters. Where AWG skips some numbers at the fine end, SWG does not — which is why "5/0" means 0.040 in in SWG but 0.0459 in in AWG.
