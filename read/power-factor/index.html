Power Factor and Three-Phase Power

Why a power factor below 1 wastes current, and how three-phase systems change the arithmetic.

Power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power. It measures how closely current and voltage stay in phase.

PF = kW / kVA PF = cos φ, where φ is the phase angle between voltage and current.
  • kWreal (useful) power
  • kVAapparent power
  • kvarreactive power
  • φphase angle

A purely resistive load draws current exactly in step with voltage, giving PF = 1. Motors, transformers and fluorescent lighting are inductive: their current lags, PF drops, and the supply carries current that does no useful work.

The power triangle

kVA² = kW² + kvar²

The three quantities form a right-angled triangle with kVA as the hypotenuse, which is why a low power factor means a large kVA for a given kW.

Three-phase

Three-phase systems do more work per ampere than single-phase, which is why industry uses them. The conversion factor depends on how the load is connected.

Single phase: kVA = V · A / 1000 Three-phase star: kVA = √3 · V · A / 1000 Three-phase delta: kVA = 3 · V · A / 1000

In star connection the line current equals the phase current and the line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage. In delta the line voltage equals the phase voltage and the line current is √3 times the phase current.

Note Utilities bill for kWh but charge penalties on low power factor, because the extra current heats the distribution network for no benefit. Correction is normally done with capacitor banks, which supply the reactive current locally.
