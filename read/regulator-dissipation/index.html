How to Calculate Linear Regulator Power Dissipation

Work out how much heat a linear regulator produces, and whether it needs a heatsink.

A linear regulator passes the excess voltage to ground as heat. The drop across the device multiplied by the current is the power it has to shed.

P = (V_in − V_out) × I_out + V_out × I_quiescent η = V_out ÷ V_in The second term is the regulator own quiescent current, usually a few milliamps - negligible on a 1 A load, but it dominates on a 10 mA load.

Worked example

A 12 V supply feeds a 5 V regulator that delivers 1 A.

P = (12 − 5) × 1 = 7 W η = 5 ÷ 12 = 42% Seven watts in a SOT-223 package is impossible; in a TO-252 with a copper area it needs a heatsink.

Heatsink sizing

θ_total ≤ (T_j,max − T_ambient) ÷ P θ_total = θ_jc + θ_cs + θ_sa θ_jc is the junction-to-case resistance of the device (about 5 °C/W for a TO-252), θ_cs the case-to-sink interface (about 1 °C/W with thermal paste) and θ_sa the sink-to-air resistance of the heatsink.

With a 100 °C junction limit, 40 °C ambient and 7 W, the total thermal resistance must be 8.6 °C/W, so the heatsink must supply about 2.6 °C/W on its own. A small 10 °C/W clip-on heatsink is plenty; a 1 °C/W extrusion is overkill.

When to switch instead

A buck converter moves the same power at 90% efficiency, so the heat drops from 7 W to 0.8 W and no heatsink is needed. The trade-off is added complexity, ripple on the output, and switching noise.

Note Watch the maximum input voltage as well as the dissipation. Many 5 V regulators are only rated to 15 V, so a 12 V automotive supply that spikes to 24 V needs a regulator with a higher rating or a pre-regulator.
