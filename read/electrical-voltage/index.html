Electrical Voltage

What voltage is, why a water analogy works, and every relationship that follows from it.

Electrical voltage is the potential difference between two points in an electric field.

Voltage is the “pressure” in a circuit. It does not itself flow — charge does. A battery does not push electrons around; it raises their potential so that a circuit can.

The water-pipe analogy is useful precisely because it is imperfect in instructive ways. Height difference is pressure, cross-section plus flow rate is current, and constriction is resistance. What the analogy misses is that water is consumed and charge is not.

V = φ₂ − φ₁ φ is the electric potential at a point, measured in volts.
  • Vvoltage between point 2 and point 1, in volts (V)
  • φ₂electric potential at point 2, in volts (V)
  • φ₁electric potential at point 1, in volts (V)

Voltage as energy per unit charge

In a circuit, the voltage across a component equals the energy it consumes divided by the charge that passes through it.

V = E / Q One volt is one joule of work per coulomb of charge.

Voltage in series and in parallel

Voltage sources or drops connected in series add up — the total is the sum of the parts.

V_T = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + …

Branches in parallel all see the same voltage, because each pair of branches shares the same two nodes.

V_T = V₁ = V₂ = V₃

The voltage divider

With resistors (or other impedances) in series, each drop is proportional to that element’s share of the total resistance.

Vᵢ = V_T · Rᵢ / (R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …)

DC and AC

Direct current comes from a constant source such as a battery; the drop across a resistor follows Ohm’s law, V_R = I_R · R.

Alternating current comes from a sinusoidal source. The same relation holds, but with impedance Z in place of resistance R, because reactive elements also shift the phase.

V_Z = I_Z · Z

A sinusoidal voltage is described by amplitude, frequency and phase.

v(t) = V_max · sin(ωt + θ) ω is the angular frequency in rad/s, t in seconds, θ in radians.

RMS and peak-to-peak

Quoted AC values are RMS, not peak. For a sine wave the relationship is fixed, which is why a 230 V supply has a 325 V peak.

V_rms = V_max / √2 ≈ 0.707 · V_max V_pp = 2 · V_max
Note A voltmeter is connected in parallel with the component being measured. It has very high resistance, so it barely perturbs the circuit it is measuring.
