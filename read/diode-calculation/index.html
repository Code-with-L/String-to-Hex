How to Calculate Diode Voltage Drop and Power

Size a rectifier diode for the current, the reverse voltage and the heat it has to shed.

A diode conducts in one direction with a characteristic forward voltage. That forward voltage is not a constant: it depends on the current, and it falls as the junction gets hotter.

Forward drop

P_diode = V_F × I_F Read V_F from the datasheet curve at your actual current - 1N4007 diodes are around 0.8 V at 1 A but closer to 0.9–1.1 V at 5 A. Schottky diodes sit at 0.3–0.5 V.

Reverse voltage

The reverse rating (PIV or VRRM) must exceed the peak reverse voltage the diode sees. For a capacitor-input rectifier that peak is roughly the peak of the AC supply, not its RMS value.

V_RRM_needed > V_peak = √2 × V_AC_rms For a 12 V AC secondary: V_peak ≈ 17 V, so a 50 V diode is comfortable Leave margin for transients and for the supply running high.

Average and repetitive current

Rectifier diodes are rated for an average forward current, but the current in a rectified waveform is a series of pulses. The repetitive peak rating (I_RRM) must cover the pulse height, and the average rating must cover the mean.

I_average ≈ 0.62 × I_peak for a full-wave rectified sine I_peak ≈ I_average × 1.57 For a 12 V 1 A supply the peak diode current is about 1.6 A, so a 1N4001 (1 A average, 3 A repetitive peak) is marginal - a 1N4004 or a Schottky is the sensible choice.

Worked example

5 V supply, 1 A output, 1N4007 at 0.9 V drop P = 0.9 × 1 = 0.9 W Dissipated heat must leave the diode body: use a DO-201 package or a Schottky at 0.4 V (0.4 W) In a linear regulator or a series element, the drop and the current multiply; in a switching converter the diode conducts only briefly and the average loss is far smaller.
Note Diode forward voltage has a negative temperature coefficient of roughly −2 mV per degree Celsius. Two diodes in a series drop can end up in thermal runaway if one carries more current than the other - keep them thermally coupled or use a matched pair.
