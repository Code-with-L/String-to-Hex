How to Calculate Resistor Power Dissipation

Work out how hot a resistor will run, then pick a package that can survive it.

Power dissipation is the rate at which a resistor converts electrical energy into heat. A resistor that dissipates more power than its package can shed will overheat, change value, or fail.

Three ways to calculate it

P = I² × R P = V² ÷ R P = V × I Use whichever pair of numbers you know for certain. I²R is the easiest when the current is set by the circuit; V²/R is easiest when the voltage across the resistor is fixed.

Pick a package with headroom

Standard packages are rated for a maximum dissipation at a specified ambient temperature, typically 25 °C: 0402 manages 1/16 W, 0603 manages 1/10 W, 0805 manages 1/8 W and 1206 manages 1/4 W at room temperature. Derate hard for hot air - a common rule is to use half the rating when the board sits above 60 °C.

Worked example

A 12 Ω resistor carries 2 A.

P = 2² × 12 = 48 W 48 W needs a wirewound power resistor in a chassis, not a surface-mount part. With a 24 W rating at 25 °C, derate to 50% for a warm enclosure and you need at least 96 W of rated capacity.
Note Pulsed loads average out: a resistor that sees 48 W for 10 ms every second dissipates far less total energy than the same current continuously. Check the average, not the peak, unless something fails catastrophically at the peak.
