How to Calculate Inductor Saturation Current and Stored Energy

Check that an inductor can carry the current and hold the energy your circuit needs.

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field. Two limits matter: the saturation current, where the inductance starts to collapse, and the RMS current, where the core losses and heating take over.

E = ½ × L × I² I_sat = the current at which L has fallen by about 20–30% I_rms = the current at which the part reaches its rated temperature Both limits are on the datasheet. Use I_sat for the worst-case peak, I_rms for continuous running.

Ripple current

In a buck or boost converter the current through the inductor is a sawtooth: an average value with a triangular ripple on top. The peak is the average plus half the ripple.

ΔI = (V_in − V_out) ÷ (L × f_sw) for a buck converter I_peak = I_average + ΔI ÷ 2 A common design rule is to keep I_peak below 80% of I_sat, leaving margin for the tolerance of the inductance value.

Worked example

A 10 µH inductor with I_sat = 4 A and I_rms = 3.2 A is used in a 5 V to 3.3 V buck converter switching at 500 kHz, delivering 1 A.

ΔI = (5 − 3.3) ÷ (10 × 10⁻⁶ × 500 000) = 0.34 A I_peak = 1 + 0.17 = 1.17 A → 29% of I_sat, comfortable E at the peak = ½ × 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.17² = 6.8 µJ The energy figure is what the switching device has to absorb when the field collapses, and what the output capacitor has to receive.
Note Saturation is not a soft limit. Once the core saturates, the inductance falls quickly, the current runs away, and the device or the inductor can be destroyed in milliseconds. Design against the datasheet minimum, not the typical value.
