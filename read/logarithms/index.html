Logarithms

What a logarithm is, the three laws, change of base and why engineers think in decades.

A logarithm answers “to what power must the base be raised to give this number?” — log_b x = y means b^y = x.

log_b x = y ⇔ b^y = x

The three laws

  • log_b (x·y)log_b x + log_b y — product becomes sum
  • log_b (x ÷ y)log_b x − log_b y — quotient becomes difference
  • log_b (xⁿ)n · log_b x — power moves out front
  • log_b 10 — any base to the zero power is 1
  • log_b b1 — the base to the first power is itself

Which base?

log means base 10 (common) in most practical work; ln means base e ≈ 2.71828 (natural, from calculus); log₂ is binary (bits, entropy, complexity). Base 2 is what “bit” means: log₂ N tells how many binary decisions separate N possibilities from one.

log₂ 256 = 8 log₁₀ 1000 = 3 ln 2 ≈ 0.693

Change of base

Any log can be rewritten in whichever base is convenient — usually to get e or 10 out of a base-2 or odd-base log.

log₂ x = ln x ÷ ln 2 = log₁₀ x ÷ log₁₀ 2

Decibels are logs

A decibel is a ratio on a logarithmic scale: 3 dB doubles the power, 10 dB multiplies it by ten. That is why audio and radio engineers never multiply — they add dB.

dB = 10 · log₁₀ (P₂ ÷ P₁)
Note Anti-log is the inverse of log: antilog_b y = b^y. The anti-log tool reverses this page’s formulas, and the log tool evaluates log_b x directly.
