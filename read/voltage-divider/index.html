How to Calculate a Voltage Divider

Split a supply voltage between two resistors, and check what the load does to the answer.

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply. The voltage across the bottom resistor is a fixed fraction of the supply, set by the ratio of the two resistances.

V_out = V_in × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂) R₁ is the top resistor (supply side), R₂ the bottom resistor (output side). The output is always measured relative to ground.

Choosing the total resistance

The formula works for any values, but the divider draws current continuously and that current loads the supply. A good default is to make the total between 10 kΩ and 100 kΩ: low enough to be immune to leakage, high enough not to waste power.

Worked example

You need 3.3 V from a 12 V rail.

R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂) = 3.3 ÷ 12 = 0.275 With R₁ = 27 kΩ: R₂ = 0.275 ÷ 0.725 × 27 kΩ = 10.2 kΩ → choose 10 kΩ V_out = 12 × 10 ÷ 37 = 3.24 V The 0.06 V error is well inside the tolerance of both 5% resistors.

The load changes the output

Anything you connect to the output is in parallel with R₂ and pulls the voltage down. The loaded divider is simply a three-resistor parallel network.

V_out_loaded = V_in × (R₂ ∥ R_load) ÷ (R₁ + (R₂ ∥ R_load)) If the load is more than about ten times R₂, the error stays below 10% and the divider can be treated as unloaded.
Note Dividers are used for biasing transistor bases, scaling sensor outputs into an ADC range, and generating a reference from a higher supply. For a precision reference use a regulator or a dedicated reference IC instead - a divider drifts with temperature and with the supply it is fed from.
