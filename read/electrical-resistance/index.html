Fundamentals

Electrical Resistance

What opposes current, how it depends on geometry and material, and how temperature changes it.

Resistance is a component’s opposition to the flow of electric current.

R = V / I One ohm passes one ampere when one volt is applied across it.

What resistance depends on

For a uniform conductor, resistance rises with length, falls with cross-sectional area, and scales with the material’s resistivity.

R = ρ · L / A ρ in Ω·m, L in m, A in m². Copper is 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 20 °C.

Doubling a wire’s length doubles its resistance; doubling its diameter quarters it, because area goes as the square.

Resistance combinations

Series: R_T = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ Parallel: 1/R_T = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

Temperature

Metals get more resistive as they heat — which is why tungsten filament lamps glow, and why a hot motor draws more current than a cold one. Carbon behaves the other way, which is how thermistors sense temperature.

Measuring resistance

An ohmmeter passes a known small current through the component and measures the resulting voltage. A component must be out of circuit, or the measurement includes everything in parallel with it.
