How to Calculate PCB Trace Width

Size a copper trace for the current it carries, its temperature rise, and the voltage it must survive.

Trace width is set by two independent limits: how much current the copper can carry before it gets too hot, and how much voltage it can hold before the insulation under it breaks down.

Current and temperature

I ≈ k × ΔT^0.44 × A^0.725 k = 0.048 for external layers and 0.024 for inner layers, A is the copper cross-section area in mil², and ΔT is the allowed temperature rise. This is the IPC-2221 external-layer relationship.

Copper area is width times thickness: 1 oz copper is 1.37 mil (35 µm) thick, 2 oz is 2.74 mil. A 20 mil wide trace on 1 oz copper has an area of 27 mil².

Worked example

ΔT = 20 °C, 1 oz copper, external layer, 20 mil wide: A = 20 × 1.37 = 27.4 mil² I = 0.048 × 20^0.44 × 27.4^0.725 ≈ 2.7 A That is with a 20 °C temperature rise. A 10 mil trace on 1 oz copper manages roughly 1.6 A at the same rise.

Voltage and creepage

The voltage limit is a dielectric one, and it depends on the distance across the board, not just the trace width. On ordinary FR-4 with a 0.1 mm ground plane underneath, a 0.25 mm trace spacing is good to about 50 V; higher voltages need wider spacing, slots in the board, or a coating.

VoltageMinimum spacing (FR-4, coated)
< 50 V0.25 mm
50–150 V0.8 mm
150–300 V1.6 mm
300–600 V3.2 mm

Practical advice

  • Copper pours on both sides of a board count as parallel conductors if they are stitched - put vias every few millimetres to join them.
  • A narrow neck in a trace counts as the whole trace for current purposes. Avoid necks near pads.
  • Thick copper (2 oz) halves the width needed for the same current and temperature rise.
Note Keep power loops small. In switching converters the loop formed by the high-current path and its return path radiates and couples into nearby signals; overlapping the two paths on adjacent layers is the usual fix.
