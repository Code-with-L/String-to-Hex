How to Calculate Energy Cost

Turn watts and hours into kilowatt-hours, cost per year, and a payback figure.

Electricity is billed per kilowatt-hour: the power a device draws multiplied by the time it is on. Almost every energy calculation is this one product.

energy (kWh) = power (W) ÷ 1000 × hours cost = kWh × price per kWh For three-phase billing the same formula applies to the real power in kW.

Worked example

A 150 W desk lamp runs 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, 48 weeks a year, at 0.30 per kWh.

per day: 0.150 kW × 8 h = 1.2 kWh per year: 1.2 × 5 × 48 = 288 kWh cost: 288 × 0.30 = 86.40 per year About 7.20 per month. Running it 24 hours a day would cost 259 per year.

Comparing two options

The saving from a more efficient device is the difference in energy, multiplied by the price, multiplied by the hours of use.

saving per year = (W_old − W_new) ÷ 1000 × hours_per_year × price payback (years) = purchase cost ÷ saving per year A 90 W LED replacing a 150 W incandescent lamp at 3 h/day, 300 days a year, 0.30 per kWh saves 16.20 per year and pays back a 6 lamp investment in about 0.4 years.

Standby and always-on loads

Devices left on 24 hours a day accumulate cost even when they look idle. A 0.5 W charger left plugged in for a year costs 0.0005 × 8760 × 0.30 = 1.31. Multiply by the number of chargers in a building and it stops being trivial.

Note Tariffs often have a time-of-use element. If the same kWh costs three times as much between 4 pm and 8 pm, shifting flexible loads - charging a vehicle, running a dryer - out of that window is worth more than buying a more efficient appliance.
