How to Save Electricity

Where the power bill actually goes, and the handful of changes that make a measurable difference.

Saving electricity means reducing the kWh drawn from the grid for the same useful work — heat, light, motion, computing — or doing major replacement to stop wasting it.

The single most important fact from your power bill: every kWh costs about the same wherever it comes from, so savings come from reducing kWh, not from consuming at a "cheaper time" — except on a time-of-use tariff, where timing shifts the price rather than the quantity.

Read the meter, not the guesswork

A plug meter shows the real draw of one appliance: standby, idle and cycling draws included. Most houses find the worst offender is something boring and always on — a dehumidifier, a set-top box, an aquarium heater, or a fridge with a tired gasket.

The changes that pay back

  • Heating and hot water: 1 °C lower on the thermostat, shorter showers, and a full drum — heat dominates most homes.
  • Stop standby: a switched extension lead costs pennies and removes idle draw.
  • Water heating: fit an insulating cylinder jacket; 60 °C is hot enough for safe legionella control.
  • Lighting: LED replacement pays for itself in a year or two of normal evening use.
  • Cold spaces: keep the fridge at 4 °C and the freezer at −18 °C; each degree colder than needed is wasted.
  • Wash cold: modern detergent is formulated for 30 °C cycles.

The maths of one appliance

A 2 kW heater running four hours a day for a year uses 2.9 MWh — more than most households spend on lighting in the same year. Any candidate for "I leave it on" is worth a plug-meter test before it becomes a habit.

kWh = kW × h Two kilowatts for four hours is eight kilowatt-hours; multiply by your unit rate to get money.
Note The biggest single saving available to most homes is insulation — loft first, then cavity. It is a one-time capital cost that pays back every winter for decades.
