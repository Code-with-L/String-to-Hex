Circuit laws

Ohm's Law

The three quantities that define a resistive circuit, and all six ways to solve for one of them.

For a conductor at constant temperature, the current through it is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends.

V = I · R Valid for ohmic components at constant temperature.

Because voltage, current and resistance are related by one equation, any two of them determine the third. The triangular arrangement below is the usual memory aid.

I = V / R V = I · R P = V · I R = V / I P = I² · R P = V² / R

Power in the three forms

  • P = V · Iuseful when voltage and current are known
  • P = I² · Ruseful when current and resistance are known
  • P = V² / Ruseful when voltage and resistance are known

Note Pick the form whose variables you have. If the circuit shows both V and I, use P = V·I. If only V and R, use P = V²/R — and note that P rises with the square of V, not with V itself.

The choice of form is not cosmetic. For a fixed-resistance load, P = V²/R means power rises with the square of voltage — halving the supply quarters the power. That is why a 12 V lamp fitted to a 6 V supply does not merely dim, it barely lights.

Where Ohm’s law stops applying

  • Semiconductor diodes and LEDs are non-ohmic — their forward voltage is nearly fixed regardless of current.
  • Filament lamps and tungsten heaters change resistance strongly with temperature.
  • In AC circuits, replace R with Z, because reactance and phase matter as well.
