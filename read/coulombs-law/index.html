Electrostatics

Coulomb's Law

The force between two point charges, and how it falls away with distance.

The electrostatic force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

F = k · q₁·q₂ / r² k = 8.9875517923×10⁹ N·m²/C² in vacuum.
  • Fforce between the charges, in newtons (N)
  • kCoulomb’s constant, 8.9875517923×10⁹ N·m²/C²
  • q₁, q₂the two charges, in coulombs (C)
  • rdistance between the charges, in metres (m)

Like charges repel, opposite charges attract — the sign of the product q₁q₂ carries that information, so the same equation covers both.

Why the inverse square

Electric field spreads over the surface of an imaginary sphere centred on the charge. Doubling the radius quadruples that surface, and since the field is spread evenly over it, the field strength falls to a quarter. Force falls off with the square of distance for the same reason gravity does.

Note Doubling the distance between two charges reduces the force to one quarter — not to one half. This is the fastest fall-off of any common force, and it is why electric forces dominate at atomic distances but are irrelevant between everyday objects.

Relation to voltage

The electric potential at a point is the work done per unit charge in bringing a test charge from infinity to that point.

V = k · q / r So V = E/Q, the same definition used in the voltage article.
