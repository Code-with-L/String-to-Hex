How to Calculate a Transistor Base Resistor

Set the operating point of a bipolar transistor with two resistors, and allow for the fact that gain is not a fixed number.

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) in common-emitter mode is biased by a base resistor that sets the base current, and a collector resistor that converts that current into a voltage drop.

Fixed bias

I_B = (V_B − V_BE) ÷ R_B I_C = β × I_B R_C = (V_CC − V_CE) ÷ I_C V_BE ≈ 0.7 V for a silicon transistor. β (h_FE) is the current gain, typically 100 to 300 but never guaranteed.

Worked example

Target: 10 mA collector current, 5 V across the collector-emitter junction, from a 12 V supply. Use a collector resistance of 680 Ω and a base resistance of 110 kΩ.

I_C = (12 − 5) ÷ 680 = 10.3 mA I_B = 10.3 mA ÷ 100 = 103 µA R_B = (12 − 0.7) ÷ 0.000103 = 110 kΩ The base resistor is the smaller of the two currents by the gain, which is why biasing is sensitive to β.

Why β makes it unreliable

The same transistor can have h_FE of 80 in one batch and 250 in the next. The collector current would swing from 7 mA to 22 mA for the identical circuit. Three fixes are used in practice:

  • Design with the minimum h_FE from the datasheet, so the worst-case transistor still stays in the active region.
  • Add an emitter resistor and split the bias between two resistors - this makes the operating point depend on resistor ratios rather than on β.
  • Use negative feedback by putting part of the emitter resistor in the emitter leg, or bias the transistor from a stable divider.

The two-resistor divider bias

V_B = V_CC × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂) R_E = (V_B − 0.7) ÷ I_E V_CE = V_CC − I_C × R_C − I_E × R_E With R_E in the emitter, the transistor tends to hold I_E roughly constant, so β stops mattering much.
Note Keep the transistor in the active region: V_CE should sit near half the supply (about 2–3 V for a small-signal stage) so the output can swing both ways without clipping.
