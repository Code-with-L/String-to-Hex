How to Calculate Resistor Values

Turn the voltage drop and current you need into a resistance, then round it to a real E-series value.

A resistor value is calculated from the voltage it must drop and the current it must pass. Everything else - tolerance, power rating, temperature coefficient - is a constraint applied to that number afterwards.

Step 1: write down what the resistor has to do

You need three numbers before any formula: the supply voltage, the voltage that should appear across the load, and the current through the load. In a series circuit the same current flows through the resistor and the load.

R = (V_supply − V_load) ÷ I V in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms. Keep milliamps in milliamps: 10 mA is 0.010 A, so 3.2 V ÷ 0.010 A = 320 Ω, not 3.2 Ω.

Step 2: round to a value that exists

Resistors are made in standard series. The E24 series has 24 values per decade with 5% steps; E96 has 96 values per decade with 1% steps; E192 has 0.5% steps. Pick the nearest standard value to your calculation and accept the resulting error.

Step 3: check the error you introduced

Error = |R_chosen − R_ideal| ÷ R_ideal × 100% Round down when you need a current limit to stay safe - a smaller resistor limits more current.

Worked example

A 5 V supply drives an LED whose forward voltage is 1.8 V, and you want about 10 mA through it.

R = (5 V − 1.8 V) ÷ 0.010 A = 320 Ω Choose 330 Ω (E24). The actual current is (5 − 1.8) ÷ 330 = 9.7 mA, about 3% below target.
Note Calculate with the highest supply voltage the circuit will ever see, not the nominal one. A 5 V USB supply can sit at 5.25 V, and that extra quarter volt changes the current directly.
