Inductor

Energy stored in a magnetic field, inductive reactance, and why a collapsing field produces a voltage spike.

An inductor stores energy in the magnetic field around a coil of wire when current flows through it.

E = ½ · L · I² L is inductance in henries, I is the current through the coil in amperes.

Voltage across an inductor

An inductor opposes every change in the current flowing through it. Voltage across it is proportional to the rate of change of current — not to the current itself.

V = L · (di ÷ dt) Cutting the current in a coil produces a fast-rising negative voltage — this is exactly how a car ignition coil makes a spark.

Inductive reactance

X_L = 2πfL

An inductor’s opposition to an AC signal rises with frequency: it passes DC freely but blocks high-frequency RF.

  • f = 50 Hz, L = 10 mHX_L ≈ 3.14 Ω
  • f = 10 kHz, L = 10 mHX_L ≈ 628 Ω
  • f = 1 MHz, L = 10 µHX_L ≈ 63 Ω

Combining inductors

Series: L_T = L₁ + L₂ + … Parallel: 1 ÷ L_T = 1 ÷ L₁ + 1 ÷ L₂ + …

Unlike capacitors, series inductors add their reactances; parallel inductors oppose each other and reduce net reactance. Parallel combination is identical to resistors.

Note A relay or transformer coil left switched open produces a very large voltage spike (V = L · di/dt) that can puncture a transistor. A freewheeling diode across the coil gives that energy a harmless path to dissipate.
