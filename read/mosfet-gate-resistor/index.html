How to Calculate MOSFET Gate Resistor and Losses

Turn a gate charge figure into a peak gate current, a switching time and a dissipation number.

A MOSFET gate behaves like a small capacitor. The charge needed to move it from off to fully on, divided by the time you allow for that move, sets the peak gate current - and that current is what the driver has to supply and the gate resistor has to limit.

The numbers you need

  • Q_g - total gate charge in nanocoulombs, from the datasheet.
  • V_GS(th) - threshold voltage, and the plateau voltage at which the MOSFET is fully enhanced.
  • C_iss - input capacitance, used for the initial turn-on step before the device starts conducting.
  • R_DS(on) - on-state resistance at the gate voltage you will actually drive.

Gate resistor

I_peak = (V_drive − V_plateau) ÷ (R_g + R_internal) t_switch ≈ Q_g ÷ I_peak R_internal is the driver output resistance. The plateau voltage is usually 4–6 V for a logic-level MOSFET.

Conduction loss

P_on = I_D² × R_DS(on) × duty cycle P_sw = V_DS × I_D × t_rise × f_sw P_on is the loss while the device is on; P_sw is the loss during the switching transitions.

Worked example

A logic-level N-MOSFET with Q_g = 12 nC and R_DS(on) = 40 mΩ switches 8 A from a 24 V rail at 20 kHz, driven from a 5 V gate driver through a 10 Ω resistor.

I_peak ≈ (5 − 4.5) ÷ 10 = 0.05 A (gate current is small for a logic-level part) t_switch ≈ 12 nC ÷ 0.05 A = 240 ns P_on = 8² × 0.04 × 0.5 = 1.28 W A 1.28 W loss at 20 kHz switching needs a copper-pad or small heatsink; the transition loss adds roughly V_DS × I_D × t_sw × f_sw = 24 × 8 × 240 ns × 20 kHz ≈ 0.9 W.
Note A gate resistor that is too small makes the switching edge fast enough to ring on the layout and to couple noise into the source. 4.7 Ω to 33 Ω is typical; go lower only when the driver is strong and the layout is tight.
