How to Calculate Wire Ampacity and Voltage Drop

Pick a wire gauge for the current it must carry and the voltage it may drop on the way.

Two separate limits apply to a cable: the maximum current it can carry without overheating its insulation, and the voltage it drops at that current. The first is a safety limit, the second a design limit.

Ampacity

Ampacity tables assume a specific installation method, ambient temperature and conductor count. Chassis wiring in free air, for example, carries more current than the same wire buried in conduit.

Voltage drop

V_drop = 2 × I × R × L ÷ 1000 The factor of two accounts for the supply and return paths. R is the resistance in mΩ per metre, L is the one-way length in metres.

Worked example

A 10 A load sits 5 m from a 12 V supply on 16 AWG cable.

V_drop = 2 × 10 × 4.02 × 5 ÷ 1000 = 0.40 V (3.4% of 12 V) Power lost = 0.40 × 10 = 4 W in the cable A 12 AWG conductor halves the drop to 0.20 V. Note that the cable also dissipates 4 W, which matters for a long run on battery power.

Derating

Ambient temperature above 30 °C, several cables sharing one conduit, and a conductor temperature close to the insulation limit all reduce the permitted current. The usual rule is to halve the table value for bundled cables in free air.

Note Voltage drop is what actually limits a long run for 12 V systems: a 12 V supply that sags to 10 V at the load can reset a microcontroller even though the cable is within its ampacity.
